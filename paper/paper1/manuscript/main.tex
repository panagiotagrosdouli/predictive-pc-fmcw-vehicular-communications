\title{When Does Future Mobility Information Have Packet-Level Value in Vehicular Optical Scheduling?}

\begin{abstract}
Trajectory prediction is increasingly used to anticipate connectivity, resource demand, and service opportunities in mobile and vehicular networks, but prediction accuracy is not itself a communication outcome. We study the downstream question of whether strictly causal future-motion information creates incremental packet-level utility in a mobility-sensitive PC-FMCW/DPSK vehicular optical scheduler with queues and hard packet deadlines. Prior work has already established trajectory prediction in dynamic VLC and trajectory-informed resource allocation in vehicular networks, so we make no first-use claim. Instead, we implement an auditable chain from observed motion history to predicted geometry, modeled optical-link evolution, scheduling, and realized packet outcomes. A final audit exposed an inclusive deadline-discretization defect; after correcting it, we prospectively re-selected the current-service guard on fresh development seeds and evaluated the selected policy once on a disjoint 20-seed synthetic holdout. The corrected protocol selected a 1.0 guard. Against Reactive Greedy, mean goodput differences in the four frozen primary regimes are 0.00000, +0.00035, -0.00050, and +0.00080 Mbps, and every comparison is NEUTRAL\_OR\_UNCERTAIN under the frozen confidence-interval/practical-margin rule. Across paired episodes, exact ties dominate (80--100\% depending on regime). Development diagnostics explain this result: looser predictive reordering can expose gains in some conditions but violates the predeclared worst-regime noninferiority gate, whereas the robust 1.0 guard protects immediate service so strongly that most incremental predictive action disappears. Thus future mobility information is not automatically packet-level value; its value depends on whether it can change a consequential service decision without sacrificing more urgent current service. The evidence is synthetic/model-based and is not presented as measured road or optical-channel validation.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Vehicular optical communication offers a directional complement to RF V2X, but its usefulness is tightly coupled to relative geometry, field of view, alignment, and mobility \cite{memedi2021vehicular}. Recent phase-coded FMCW laser-headlamp work further motivates architectures in which sensing, illumination, and communication share an optical platform \cite{liu2026laserheadlamp,kumbul2023smoothed,kumbul2023mimo}. These characteristics make future vehicle motion an apparently valuable scheduling signal: a link that is strong now may soon deteriorate, while a purely reactive scheduler sees only the present opportunity.

That intuition is plausible but insufficient. Prior research has already used trajectory prediction in dynamic narrow-field-of-view VLC \cite{jiang2020trajectory}; vehicular VLC resource allocation has explicitly considered connectivity, load balancing, and network lifetime \cite{msongaleli2020resource}; and trajectory prediction has been incorporated into LTE-V2V resource selection and vehicular computation/resource allocation \cite{hajrasouliha2021trajectory,guo2022trajectoryoffload,lu2024predictive}. Low-latency/reliable vehicular resource allocation also predates this work \cite{guo2019latency}. Therefore, neither trajectory prediction, mobility-aware resource allocation, nor their use in vehicular systems is itself the contribution here.

A narrower downstream question remains important: when a causal mobility forecast is translated through link prediction into a scheduling decision, does it change the fate of packets with queues and deadlines? This distinction matters because trajectory error, link-state error, scheduler disagreement, and packet utility are different objects. A forecast may accurately describe future geometry yet be redundant with current link state; it may change a ranking without changing a successful service; or it may encourage service of a deteriorating link at the cost of a better immediate transmission.

We therefore ask: \emph{when does causal future-motion information have packet-level communication value in mobility-sensitive vehicular optical scheduling, and when does that value disappear?} We study this question in a model-based PC-FMCW/DPSK setting using deployable causal predictors, evaluator-only future ground truth, explicit queues and deadlines, paired episode-level inference, and a prospectively selected service guard. Our purpose is mechanism identification, not a universal predictive-superiority claim.

The principal findings are deliberately conditional. First, after correcting packet-deadline semantics and repeating development selection on fresh seeds, the robust rule selects a 1.0 current-service guard. Second, on the disjoint corrected holdout, none of four predeclared regimes establishes practically positive or negative goodput relative to Reactive Greedy. Third, the paired outcomes are mostly exact ties. Finally, development ablations reveal the mechanism behind that neutrality: relaxing the guard permits more predictive reordering and some positive changes, but also creates a short-deadline loss that makes those candidates ineligible. Prediction can therefore contain information without yielding robust incremental packet utility.

Our contributions are: (i) an auditable cross-layer evaluation that separates causal motion forecasting, predicted optical-link evolution, scheduler decisions, and realized packet outcomes; (ii) a corrected paired holdout result showing no robust incremental primary goodput advantage for the prospectively selected guarded policy; and (iii) a mechanism analysis showing that the constraint needed to prevent harmful predictive reordering can also collapse the actionability of future information. The claim is intentionally narrower than priority or state-of-the-art claims.

\section{Related Work}
\subsection{Vehicular optical communication}
Vehicular VLC research establishes the opportunity and constraints created by directional vehicle lighting, line-of-sight geometry, mobility, and optical channel dynamics \cite{memedi2021vehicular}. Msongaleli and Kucuk formulate resource utilisation for VLC-based VANETs around network lifetime, connectivity, and load balancing, using an ILP and a heuristic for denser cases \cite{msongaleli2020resource}. These works make clear that mobility-sensitive resource decisions in vehicular optical networks are not new. Our question differs by tracing causal motion information through a packet scheduler with explicit deadlines and asking for incremental packet-level utility against a strong current-state baseline.

\subsection{Trajectory prediction as communication side information}
Trajectory prediction has already entered VLC. Jiang \emph{et al.} study target-light-source trajectory prediction for a dynamic VLC system with a narrow field of view \cite{jiang2020trajectory}. In RF V2V, Hajrasouliha and Shahgholi Ghahfarokhi use predicted vehicle locations to drive geo-based LTE-V2V resource selection, reporting improvements in packet reception and blocking-related metrics under their evaluated settings \cite{hajrasouliha2021trajectory}. In vehicular edge systems, trajectory prediction has likewise been coupled to task offloading and resource allocation \cite{guo2022trajectoryoffload,lu2024predictive}. Consequently, this paper does not claim that forecasting vehicle motion for communication decisions is unprecedented.

\subsection{Prediction quality versus downstream utility}
Vehicle trajectory forecasting itself is a mature problem; for example, interacting-multiple-model methods combine motion hypotheses for interpretable prediction \cite{gao2023imm}. Communication systems, however, ultimately care about service outcomes rather than displacement error alone. The relevant causal chain is motion history $\rightarrow$ forecast $\rightarrow$ predicted link state $\rightarrow$ scheduling action $\rightarrow$ realized packet outcome. An improvement at an upstream stage need not propagate through every downstream stage. This paper evaluates that propagation directly and preserves evaluator-only future truth as an information reference rather than deployable scheduler input.

\subsection{Positioning}
The closest prior work collectively establishes the ingredients that would make a broad novelty claim untenable: dynamic VLC trajectory prediction \cite{jiang2020trajectory}, VLC-VANET resource allocation \cite{msongaleli2020resource}, trajectory-informed V2V resource selection \cite{hajrasouliha2021trajectory}, and predictive vehicular offloading/resource allocation \cite{guo2022trajectoryoffload,lu2024predictive}. Our contribution is therefore not another assertion that prediction can be inserted into a scheduler. We ask whether future mobility information remains \emph{actionable at packet level} once immediate service opportunity, queues, deadlines, and robustness across operating regimes are imposed simultaneously.

\section{System Model}
At decision slot $t$, the scheduler observes vehicle histories only through $t$, packet queues, deadlines, and past service. A causal predictor produces future target positions over horizon $H$. Ego-relative range and bearing are mapped through the analytical optical-link model to future SNR, BER/PER, goodput, outage, and link-lifetime estimates. Realized packet outcomes use ground-truth-derived geometry only at the realized transmission slot; future truth is never supplied to deployable policies.

The Part-A physical-layer reference supplies the PC-FMCW/DPSK premise and reference parameters. Phase-coded FMCW waveform/transceiver literature supports the waveform family \cite{kumbul2023smoothed,kumbul2023mimo}, while the laser-headlamp reference motivates integrated optical sensing/communication/illumination \cite{liu2026laserheadlamp}. The end-to-end geometry-to-link mapping used here nevertheless remains an analytical simulation model. Absolute received power is not calibrated to measured vehicle-to-vehicle watts.

Packets arrive into per-receiver queues and can be retransmitted after failed service attempts. An $N$-slot physical deadline permits exactly $N$ service opportunities; the inclusive final legal service index is $arrival+N-1$. Physical deadlines not exactly representable by the simulator slot duration are rejected rather than rounded. Queue conservation accounts for delivery, expiration, retransmission, drops, and right-censored packets.

\section{Scheduling and Information References}
For receiver $i$, a predictive scheduler evaluates a utility
\begin{equation}
 i^{\star}=\arg\max_i U_i\!\left(\mathcal{H}_t,Q_t,D_t,\widehat{\mathcal{L}}_{i,t+1:t+H}\right),
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{H}_t$ is causal motion history, $Q_t$ and $D_t$ represent queue/deadline state, and $\widehat{\mathcal{L}}$ is predicted link evolution. Link-lifetime urgency can increase priority when a currently usable receiver is predicted to lose service.

This creates a fundamental trade-off. A forecast-driven candidate can be urgent in the future while being inferior for immediate service. The current-service guard allows predictive reordering only when the predictive candidate preserves a specified fraction of the immediate service score of the reactive choice. The corrected development family evaluates guard ratios 0.8, 0.9, 0.95, and 1.0; the frozen corrected-development choice is \texttt{service\_guarded\_100}. At 1.0, predictive reordering cannot accept a lower immediate service score under the guard definition.

Reactive baselines include Random, Round Robin, Reactive Greedy, and Proportional Fair. Causal motion references include Last Position, constant velocity, constant acceleration, Kalman-CV, and IMM-style forecasting where configured. Oracle future positions, where present in repository diagnostics, are evaluator-side information references only: they are neither deployable nor claimed to be a globally optimal offline scheduler.

\section{Experimental and Statistical Protocol}
The corrected confirmatory protocol was frozen after discovery of the deadline-discretization defect. The historical implementation allowed $N+1$ opportunities for an intended $N$-slot deadline and also contained a nominal 0.05 s regime that is not exactly representable at 0.1 s/slot. Historical results affected by those semantics remain provenance only.

Corrected development uses seeds 20270101--20270110. Corrected holdout uses disjoint seeds 20270201--20270220 and was evaluated after development selection. Four primary regimes were fixed: deadline 0.1 s, deadline 0.5 s, offered load 1.1, and reference SNR +3 dB. The primary endpoint is paired candidate-minus-Reactive goodput. The independent inferential unit is the paired scenario/episode seed, not packets, windows, or time samples.

The frozen analysis uses 100,000 paired percentile bootstrap replicates for the mean paired difference, a 95\% confidence interval, a two-sided paired Wilcoxon signed-rank test, Holm correction across the four primary comparisons, paired Cohen $d_z$, and paired win/loss/tie fractions. With practical margin $m=0.001$ Mbps, HELP requires the confidence-interval lower bound to exceed $+m$, HURT requires the upper bound to be below $-m$, and all remaining outcomes are NEUTRAL\_OR\_UNCERTAIN. A non-significant test is not interpreted as proof of equality.

The 0.1 s regime contains 20 exact paired ties. At 0.5 s, 95\% of pairs tie and 5\% favor the guarded policy. At load 1.1, 85\% tie, 10\% favor and 5\% disfavor it. At +3 dB, 80\% tie, 15\% favor and 5\% disfavor it. The selected policy therefore realizes almost the same packet-level goodput as Reactive Greedy in most paired episodes.

For the all-zero 0.1 s contrast, the Wilcoxon statistic is degenerate; the publication analysis records the raw test as undefined and conservatively assigns Holm-adjusted $p=1$. The other raw Wilcoxon $p$ values are 0.3173, 1.0, and 0.2733, and every Holm-adjusted value is 1.0. These results indicate uncertainty and small observed effects; they are not evidence that the policies are mathematically identical.

\subsection{RQ2: Why does future information fail to produce robust incremental utility?}
Corrected development ablations expose a service-order trade-off. Guard 0.8 produces positive development mean goodput changes in the 0.5 s and +3 dB regimes but a negative mean at the 0.1 s deadline, making it ineligible under the predeclared worst-regime noninferiority rule. Guards 0.9 and 0.95 also fail that gate. Only guard 1.0 is eligible and is selected before holdout inspection.

This is evidence of an actionability bottleneck. Looser guards allow future-link urgency to alter service ordering, creating both opportunities and harms across regimes. The robust 1.0 guard removes the harmful immediate-service sacrifice, but it also removes much of the scheduler's freedom to exploit future information. The 80--100\% holdout tie rates are consistent with that mechanism: access to a forecast does not guarantee that the robust scheduler can take a different consequential action.

\subsection{RQ3: What can and cannot be concluded about prediction value?}
The corrected evidence rejects the historical claim that the selected predictive policy improves three of four primary regimes. It also rejects a universal statement that prediction is useless. Development diagnostics show that predictive reordering can change outcomes, but the direction is regime dependent. The confirmatory result is narrower: under the frozen robust selection criterion, the policy that survives cross-regime noninferiority does not demonstrate robust incremental packet-level goodput on the corrected holdout.

The distinction between information and utility is therefore central. Future geometry can be informative; a link prediction can be accurate; and a predictive ranking can differ from a reactive one. Packet-level value arises only if that information causes a service decision whose realized benefit exceeds the opportunity cost imposed elsewhere in the queue/deadline system.

\section{Discussion}
\subsection{Prediction accuracy is not communication value}
Trajectory forecasting literature typically evaluates geometric prediction quality, while resource-allocation work often demonstrates benefits within a particular optimization objective \cite{gao2023imm,hajrasouliha2021trajectory,guo2022trajectoryoffload,lu2024predictive}. Our corrected result illustrates why those levels should not be conflated. A scheduler consumes a forecast through an action-selection rule; the forecast has packet value only through the action it changes and the packet outcome that follows.

\subsection{Link urgency and packet urgency need not coincide}
Predicted link deterioration creates \emph{link urgency}; approaching packet expiration creates \emph{packet urgency}. They need not rank receivers identically. Serving a link because it will disappear soon can be harmful when another queue has a stronger immediate transmission or a more urgent packet. The corrected guard-selection pattern is consistent with this tension, although the present frozen evidence does not by itself quantify a universal relationship between the two urgency notions. We therefore treat that relationship as a mechanism hypothesis rather than a proven general law.

\subsection{Why the null/mixed result matters}
A null/mixed result is informative here because the development rule was designed to reject a predictor that gains in one regime by creating unacceptable loss in another. The resulting policy is robust but nearly reactive in realized primary utility. For system design, this suggests that adding a trajectory predictor is insufficient; the scheduler needs a decision interface that identifies when forecast information is both trustworthy and worth acting on relative to immediate packet service.

\section{Limitations}
The evidence is synthetic/model-based. The optical channel uses analytical reference-SNR/geometry assumptions rather than measured end-to-end vehicular optical calibration. No road deployment, measured optical channel, or hardware-in-the-loop experiment is claimed. The Part-A waveform work provides physical-layer provenance but does not validate the complete geometry-to-packet simulator.

The study evaluates a finite scheduler family and four frozen primary operating regimes. The 1.0 guard is not globally optimal. The corrected confirmatory evidence does not yet provide a dense operating-region map, a calibrated forecast-error sensitivity curve, or a globally optimal oracle upper bound; such analyses would be secondary diagnostics and must not be retroactively represented as primary evidence. The current mechanism interpretation is therefore strongest for the observed guard-selection and paired tie structure.

Learned GRU/WOMD work belongs to Paper 2 and is excluded from Paper 1's claims. External validity remains limited until the mechanism is tested with measured channels, broader mobility conditions, and independent data.

\section{Conclusion}
Prior literature already demonstrates trajectory prediction in dynamic VLC and trajectory-informed resource decisions in vehicular networks; the relevant question is not whether prediction can be inserted into the communication stack, but when its information survives the stack and becomes packet utility. In the corrected Paper-1 protocol, prospective development selection chooses a 1.0 current-service guard. On a disjoint 20-seed synthetic holdout, all four frozen primary goodput comparisons against Reactive Greedy are NEUTRAL\_OR\_UNCERTAIN, with 80--100\% exact paired ties. Development ablations show why: allowing more predictive reordering exposes both potential gains and cross-regime losses, while the robust guard protects immediate service so strongly that most incremental predictive action disappears. The resulting lesson is conditional rather than universal: future mobility information has packet-level value only when it can change a consequential service decision without imposing a larger immediate queue/deadline opportunity cost.
