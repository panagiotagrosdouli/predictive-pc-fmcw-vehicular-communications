\title{When Does Future Mobility Information Have Packet-Level Value in Vehicular Optical Scheduling?}
\author{\IEEEauthorblockN{Panagiota Grosdouli}\IEEEauthorblockA{Department of Electrical and Computer Engineering\\School of Engineering\\Democritus University of Thrace\\Xanthi, Greece\\panagros1@ee.duth.gr}}

\begin{abstract}
Mobility prediction can reveal that a vehicular optical link is about to deteriorate, but the forecast is useful only if acting on it improves packet service. We study this distinction in a PC-FMCW/DPSK vehicular optical system with causal motion prediction, time-varying link quality, explicit queues, and hard packet deadlines. Predicted motion is converted into future link estimates and used by a guarded predictive scheduler; realized future positions are never available to the policy. The scheduler is compared with Reactive Greedy in four pre-specified operating regimes using 20 paired holdout episodes per regime. Mean goodput differences are 0.00000, +0.00035, -0.00050, and +0.00080 Mbps for deadlines of 0.1 and 0.5 s, offered load 1.1, and reference SNR +3 dB, respectively. None establishes a practically meaningful positive or negative effect under the pre-specified analysis, and 80--100\% of paired episodes have identical goodput. Development experiments with less restrictive service guards show why: allowing prediction to alter service order creates gains at some operating points but introduces a loss under the shortest deadline. Thus, future-state information and packet-level value are not equivalent. Prediction becomes useful when it changes a feasible scheduling action and the benefit of that change exceeds its immediate service cost.
\end{abstract}

\begin{IEEEkeywords}
vehicular optical communications, predictive scheduling, trajectory prediction, PC-FMCW, DPSK, packet deadlines

\section{Introduction}
Vehicular optical communication is unusually sensitive to motion. Range, relative orientation, incidence angle, field of view, and line-of-sight geometry can change over the duration of a short vehicle encounter, so the service rate available to a receiver is inherently time varying \cite{memedi2021vehicular}. Phase-coded FMCW work and the proposed laser-headlamp architecture further motivate vehicle platforms in which optical sensing and communication coexist \cite{kumbul2023smoothed,kumbul2023mimo,liu2026laserheadlamp}. In such a system, a motion forecast can indicate that a transmission opportunity is likely to disappear before the scheduler observes the deterioration directly.

That observation leads to a natural scheduling idea: serve a receiver before its predicted link loss. The difficulty is that packets, not links, are the objects that ultimately succeed or expire. A receiver with a deteriorating link may have ample deadline slack, while another queue may contain a packet that must be served immediately. Likewise, preserving a future opportunity may require abandoning a stronger transmission in the current slot. The communication value of a forecast therefore depends on a downstream decision: whether the scheduler should change service order because of information about the future.

Mobility prediction has already been used in communication problems. Trajectory prediction has been studied for narrow-field-of-view VLC \cite{jiang2020trajectory}; vehicular VLC resource allocation has considered connectivity, load balancing, and network lifetime \cite{msongaleli2020resource}; predicted positions have informed LTE-V2V resource selection \cite{hajrasouliha2021trajectory}; and trajectory-aware or predictive decisions appear in vehicular computation and resource allocation \cite{guo2022trajectoryoffload,lu2024predictive}. Reliable low-latency V2V allocation has separately established the importance of packet-level timing constraints \cite{guo2019latency}. These lines of work motivate prediction-aware control, but they leave a narrower cross-layer question: when does additional future-motion information survive the scheduling decision and produce a measurable packet-level benefit?

We address that question by evaluating prediction at the point where it can affect packet service. Causal motion histories generate future geometry and optical-link estimates. A predictive scheduler may use those estimates to change receiver priority, subject to a guard on the immediate service it is allowed to sacrifice. The guard is selected on development episodes and then evaluated once on a disjoint holdout against Reactive Greedy. This design separates the availability of predictive information from the value of acting on it.

The contribution is not a new trajectory predictor or a claim that prediction universally improves vehicular scheduling. Rather, the paper provides a packet-level test of the prediction-to-action pathway. It (i) connects causal motion forecasts to mobility-sensitive optical service under explicit queues and deadlines, (ii) measures incremental goodput with paired holdout inference across deadline, load, and SNR stressors, and (iii) uses service-guard ablations and paired outcome ties to identify where predictive information ceases to affect packet utility. The central finding is a constraint on predictive scheduling: information has little downstream value when the feasible action set or the cost of changing the current action prevents the scheduler from exploiting it.

\section{Related Work}
Vehicular VLC links communication performance directly to road geometry, alignment, line of sight, and the directional characteristics of vehicle lighting \cite{memedi2021vehicular}. Resource allocation in VLC-based vehicular networks has consequently considered connectivity, load distribution, and network lifetime \cite{msongaleli2020resource}. The PC-FMCW literature provides a complementary physical-layer motivation: smoothed and coherent MIMO phase-coded FMCW waveforms have been studied for sensing-oriented operation \cite{kumbul2023smoothed,kumbul2023mimo}, and a phase-coded FMCW laser-headlamp has been proposed for integrated sensing, communication, and illumination \cite{liu2026laserheadlamp}. Our use of this literature is as a physical-layer premise for a mobility-sensitive system model, not as experimental validation of the end-to-end simulator.

Prediction has also been brought directly into communication decisions. Jiang \emph{et al.} use trajectory prediction to track a light source in dynamic narrow-field-of-view VLC \cite{jiang2020trajectory}. Hajrasouliha and Shahgholi Ghahfarokhi use predicted vehicle locations for geo-based LTE-V2V resource selection \cite{hajrasouliha2021trajectory}, while trajectory-aware offloading and predictive resource allocation have been studied in vehicular edge systems \cite{guo2022trajectoryoffload,lu2024predictive}. Vehicle-state prediction itself can combine multiple motion hypotheses, as in interacting-multiple-model estimation \cite{gao2023imm}.

The distinction made here is between forecast quality and downstream packet utility. A forecast can improve an estimate of future geometry without changing the receiver selected for service. A changed receiver can still leave delivered goodput unchanged, and a forecast-driven action can be harmful if it sacrifices a more urgent or more productive current transmission. We therefore treat the relevant chain as
\begin{equation}
\text{motion history}\rightarrow\text{forecast}\rightarrow\text{link estimate}\rightarrow\text{service action}\rightarrow\text{packet outcome}.
\label{eq:chain}
\end{equation}
The experiments ask where, along this chain, predictive information stops producing incremental value.

\section{System and Scheduling Model}
\subsection{Causal information and packet service}
Time is slotted with duration $T_s=0.1$ s. At slot $t$, a deployable scheduler observes vehicle motion only through $t$, together with the current packet queues, packet deadlines, and previous service outcomes. A causal predictor estimates target positions over a horizon $H$. Predicted ego-relative range and bearing are mapped by an analytical optical model to quantities including SNR, BER/PER, goodput, outage state, and remaining link lifetime. The outcome of an actual transmission is evaluated from geometry realized in the transmission slot. Realized future positions are reserved for evaluator-side diagnostics and are never exposed to the scheduling policy.

The optical model follows a PC-FMCW/DPSK premise informed by \cite{kumbul2023smoothed,kumbul2023mimo,liu2026laserheadlamp}. It supplies a geometry-dependent service process rather than a calibrated hardware channel. Accordingly, the results concern scheduler behavior in the modeled system and should not be read as measured vehicle-to-vehicle optical performance.

Each receiver has a packet queue. An unsuccessful transmission may be retried while its packet remains valid. A deadline of $N$ slots gives exactly $N$ legal service opportunities; for a packet arriving in slot $a$, the last admissible service slot is $a+N-1$. Deadlines not representable by an integer number of simulation slots are excluded. Delivery, expiration, retransmission, drop, and right-censoring are accounted for separately.

\subsection{Predictive service and the current-service guard}
Let $\mathcal H_t$ denote causal motion history and let $Q_t$ and $D_t$ denote queue and deadline state. For receiver $i$, the predictive policy evaluates
\begin{equation}
U_i\!\left(\mathcal H_t,Q_t,D_t,\widehat{\mathcal L}_{i,t+1:t+H}\right),
\end{equation}
where $\widehat{\mathcal L}_{i,t+1:t+H}$ is the forecast-derived link evolution. A receiver whose usable link is expected to disappear may therefore receive additional priority before the predicted loss occurs.

Forecast-driven priority is constrained by a current-service guard. Let $S_i(t)$ be the immediate service score, $i_R$ the receiver preferred by the reactive rule, $i_P$ the forecast-driven candidate, and $r$ the guard ratio. The predictive alternative is admissible only when
\begin{equation}
S_{i_P}(t)\ge r\,S_{i_R}(t).
\label{eq:guard}
\end{equation}
Thus $r$ controls how much immediate service score may be exchanged for a future link opportunity. Development considers $r\in\{0.8,0.9,0.95,1.0\}$. The cross-regime selection rule chooses $r=1.0$, which prevents forecast-driven replacement by a receiver with a lower immediate score under Eq.~\eqref{eq:guard}.

Random, Round Robin, Reactive Greedy, and Proportional Fair are retained as reactive references. Causal motion references include Last Position, constant velocity, constant acceleration, Kalman-CV, and IMM-style forecasting where applicable. Future-position diagnostics are information references only; they are neither deployable policies nor claims of globally optimal offline scheduling.

\section{Experimental Methodology}
\subsection{Development and holdout design}
Scheduler development uses seeds 20270101--20270110. The primary evaluation uses the disjoint seeds 20270201--20270220. For each seed, candidate and Reactive-Greedy policies are evaluated on matched mobility and traffic episodes, so inference is based on paired episode-level differences rather than on packets or time samples treated as independent observations.

Four primary regimes represent distinct scheduling pressures: a 0.1 s packet deadline, a 0.5 s packet deadline, offered load 1.1, and reference SNR +3 dB. The primary endpoint is candidate-minus-Reactive-Greedy packet goodput. Guard selection is completed using development episodes before holdout results are examined. Lower guards that improve selected development conditions are not retained when they violate the pre-specified worst-regime noninferiority requirement.

The 0.1 s slot duration also fixes which deadlines are meaningful. The reported experiments use the corrected convention that an $N$-slot deadline provides exactly $N$ service opportunities. A previously explored 0.05 s deadline is not representable at this slot duration and is not part of the reported evidence.

\subsection{Statistical analysis}
For each primary regime, the mean paired goodput difference is reported with a 95\% percentile-bootstrap confidence interval from 100,000 paired replicates. A two-sided paired Wilcoxon signed-rank test is accompanied by Holm correction across the four primary comparisons. Paired Cohen $d_z$ and win/loss/tie fractions describe effect magnitude and episode-level behavior.

The practical margin is fixed at $m=0.001$ Mbps. A positive effect requires the lower confidence bound to exceed $+m$; a negative effect requires the upper confidence bound to fall below $-m$. Otherwise the result is classified as neutral/uncertain with respect to a practically meaningful effect. This criterion is deliberately distinct from a null-hypothesis test: failure to reject a zero difference is not interpreted as proof that the schedulers are equivalent.

The paired outcomes are dominated by exact ties. At the 0.1 s deadline, all 20 paired episodes have the same goodput. At 0.5 s, 95\% tie and 5\% favor the predictive scheduler. At offered load 1.1, 85\% tie, 10\% favor the predictive scheduler, and 5\% favor Reactive Greedy. At +3 dB, the corresponding fractions are 80\%, 15\%, and 5\%. The small mean differences therefore arise from relatively few episodes in which the two policies produce different packet-level outcomes.

The all-zero contrast at 0.1 s makes the raw Wilcoxon statistic undefined; its Holm-adjusted value is conservatively assigned $p=1$. Raw Wilcoxon $p$ values for the remaining regimes are 0.3173, 1.0, and 0.2733, and all Holm-adjusted values equal 1.0. The confidence intervals, effect sizes, and multiplicity-adjusted tests provide no basis for claiming an incremental goodput advantage or loss for the selected predictive policy.

\subsection{What changes when the guard is relaxed?}
The development ablations expose the trade-off hidden by the conservative selected policy. With $r=0.8$, mean development goodput increases in the 0.5 s and +3 dB regimes but decreases under the 0.1 s deadline. This short-deadline loss violates the cross-regime selection requirement. The 0.9 and 0.95 settings also fail the worst-regime requirement, leaving $r=1.0$ as the eligible configuration before holdout evaluation.

A lower guard gives forecast-derived link urgency more influence over service order. This can preserve a receiver whose link is expected to deteriorate, but the reordering consumes an immediate opportunity elsewhere. Tight deadlines make that opportunity difficult to recover. At $r=1.0$, the immediate sacrifice is prevented, but prediction can change service only when the forecast-driven candidate is already competitive in current service score. The selected policy therefore avoids the observed short-deadline loss at the cost of reducing the set of decisions on which prediction can act.

\subsection{From information to action}
Figure~\ref{fig:pathway} summarizes the mechanism suggested by the two sets of evidence. The guard ablations establish that changing the influence of prediction can change packet outcomes, while the 80--100\% holdout tie rates show that the selected conservative policy usually produces the same episode-level goodput as Reactive Greedy. These observations are consistent with an actionability bottleneck between forecast and service action.

The evidence does not show that mobility prediction is generally ineffective, nor does it prove that every tie results from an identical slot-by-slot action sequence. Rather, it identifies a narrower boundary in the evaluated scheduler: future information must first create a feasible alternative to the reactive action, and that alternative must improve packet service enough to repay its immediate opportunity cost. When either condition fails, additional future-state information does not translate into incremental goodput.

\section{Discussion}
The results distinguish forecast accuracy, scheduling action, and packet utility. These are related but not interchangeable quantities. A better motion estimate improves the information available to the scheduler. Communication performance changes only if that information alters a consequential action. This distinction matters especially under deadlines because link urgency and packet urgency can point to different receivers: a link may be about to disappear while another queue contains the packet with the least remaining slack.

The selected guard resolves this conflict conservatively. That choice is useful for interpreting the null/mixed primary result. The high tie fractions are not evidence that future geometry contains no information; they show that, under the selected protection of immediate service, the information rarely changes realized episode goodput. Conversely, the lower-guard development behavior shows that making prediction more influential is not automatically beneficial. More predictive action creates both opportunity and exposure to harmful reordering.

This suggests a scheduler-design question beyond the present experiment. Instead of applying one fixed willingness to sacrifice current service, a predictive scheduler could estimate whether a particular state is actionable using forecast confidence, expected loss of future link opportunity, queue occupancy, and remaining deadline slack. Such a design is a hypothesis motivated by the present results, not a method evaluated here. Testing it would require direct instrumentation of prediction-induced action changes and a new confirmatory evaluation.

\section{Limitations}
The study is simulation based. Optical service is generated from analytical geometry and reference-SNR assumptions rather than from a measured end-to-end vehicular optical channel. The PC-FMCW/DPSK literature motivates the physical-layer setting, but the complete geometry-to-packet model has not been validated in a road deployment or hardware-in-the-loop experiment.

The primary evaluation contains four operating regimes and 20 paired holdout episodes per regime. It covers a finite scheduler family and does not establish that the selected guard is globally optimal. It also does not provide a dense operating-region map over mobility, traffic, SNR, deadline, and forecast-error conditions. Finally, the mechanism interpretation relies on guard ablations and episode-level outcome ties; the current experiment does not log and classify every individual prediction-induced scheduling change.

Measured optical channels, broader mobility and traffic distributions, learned trajectory models, larger driving datasets, and direct decision-change instrumentation would allow the actionability hypothesis to be tested under a wider range of conditions.

\section{Conclusion}
Causal knowledge of future motion does not by itself guarantee better packet service. In the vehicular optical system studied here, the predictive scheduler selected to protect immediate service establishes no practically meaningful goodput advantage or loss relative to Reactive Greedy across four primary regimes. Mean differences range from -0.00050 to +0.00080 Mbps, while 80--100\% of paired holdout episodes have identical goodput. Development ablations show the complementary result: relaxing the service guard gives prediction more influence and can improve some operating points, but it also introduces a loss under the shortest deadline. Together, these results locate the limiting step between forecast and action. Future mobility information has packet-level value when it changes a feasible service decision and the resulting benefit exceeds the immediate cost of taking that decision.
