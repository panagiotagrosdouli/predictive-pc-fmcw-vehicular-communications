\title{When Does Future Mobility Information Have Packet-Level Value in Vehicular Optical Scheduling?}
\author{\IEEEauthorblockN{Panagiota Grosdouli}\IEEEauthorblockA{Department of Electrical and Computer Engineering\\School of Engineering\\Democritus University of Thrace\\Xanthi, Greece\\panagros1@ee.duth.gr}}

\begin{abstract}
A mobility forecast can anticipate the loss of a vehicular optical link, but this information is useful only if a scheduler can exploit it without giving up a more valuable transmission in the present. We study this tension in a PC-FMCW/DPSK vehicular optical system with causal motion prediction, time-varying link quality, packet queues, and hard deadlines. Predicted vehicle motion is mapped to future optical-link conditions and used to influence packet service, while realized future positions remain unavailable to the scheduler. We compare a guarded predictive policy with Reactive Greedy over four operating regimes using 20 paired holdout episodes per regime. The mean goodput differences are 0.00000, +0.00035, -0.00050, and +0.00080 Mbps for deadlines of 0.1 and 0.5 s, offered load 1.1, and reference SNR +3 dB, respectively. None provides evidence of a practically meaningful goodput advantage, and 80--100\% of paired episodes produce identical goodput. Additional guard experiments show the underlying trade-off: giving the forecast more influence can improve some operating points, but can also reduce goodput when packet deadlines are tight. The results show that forecast availability alone is insufficient. Packet-level benefit depends on whether future link information changes a feasible service decision whose eventual benefit exceeds the cost of not taking the best immediate action.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Vehicular optical links are strongly shaped by motion. Relative position and orientation determine range, incidence angle, field of view, and line-of-sight availability, so a link that is usable at one instant may deteriorate rapidly as the vehicles move \cite{memedi2021vehicular}. Optical vehicle platforms that combine illumination, sensing, and communication make this dependence especially relevant \cite{liu2026laserheadlamp,kumbul2023smoothed,kumbul2023mimo}. A scheduler that knows how the geometry is likely to evolve could, in principle, serve a receiver before a favorable transmission opportunity disappears.

The difficulty is deciding when that future opportunity is worth acting on. Packet scheduling is not performed on link state alone. Packets wait in different queues, their remaining lifetimes differ, and only one of several competing transmissions may be selected. Serving a receiver early because its link is predicted to deteriorate can preserve a later outage from causing a loss. The same choice can also be counterproductive if it displaces a stronger current transmission or delays a packet with less deadline slack. A forecast can therefore be informative without being useful to the packet scheduler.

Prior work provides strong motivation for mobility-aware communication. Trajectory prediction has been considered in dynamic narrow-field-of-view VLC \cite{jiang2020trajectory}, while vehicular VLC resource allocation has addressed connectivity, load balancing, and network lifetime \cite{msongaleli2020resource}. Predicted vehicle locations have also been used in LTE-V2V resource selection \cite{hajrasouliha2021trajectory}, and mobility prediction has been coupled with task offloading and resource allocation in vehicular edge networks \cite{guo2022trajectoryoffload,lu2024predictive}. Reliable low-latency vehicular resource allocation has been studied independently of this predictive setting \cite{guo2019latency}. These results establish the usefulness of mobility information in several communication problems, but they do not imply that every improvement in future-state knowledge produces a packet-level gain under queues and deadlines.

This paper studies the point at which predictive information becomes operationally useful. Causal motion histories are converted into predicted geometry and link evolution. A packet scheduler may use those estimates to alter service order, but it must do so while accounting for the value of the transmission available now. We evaluate the resulting policy against a strong reactive scheduler and examine not only average goodput differences, but also how often the additional future information changes the realized packet outcome.

The contribution is threefold. First, we formulate a cross-layer evaluation in which causal vehicle-motion forecasts propagate through an optical-link model to deadline-constrained packet scheduling. Second, we quantify the incremental value of predictive scheduling with paired holdout experiments across deadline, load, and SNR conditions. Third, we examine the mechanism behind the observed performance through service-guard ablations and episode-level ties. The experiments reveal a practical boundary: prediction has little packet-level effect when the scheduler cannot safely translate the forecast into a different service action.

\section{Related Work}
\subsection{Mobility and vehicular optical communication}
Vehicular VLC combines highly directional optical propagation with rapidly changing road geometry. The survey in \cite{memedi2021vehicular} describes the resulting sensitivity to line of sight, alignment, mobility, and vehicle-lighting characteristics. Msongaleli and Kucuk study resource utilisation in VLC-based vehicular ad hoc networks, optimizing network lifetime, connectivity, and load distribution through an ILP formulation and a lower-complexity heuristic \cite{msongaleli2020resource}. These studies motivate resource management that responds to mobility, although their objectives differ from the deadline-constrained packet service considered here.

Phase-coded FMCW techniques provide the waveform context for the optical system used in this work. Smoothed phase-coded FMCW and coherent MIMO phase-coded FMCW have been studied for sensing-oriented waveform design \cite{kumbul2023smoothed,kumbul2023mimo}, while a phase-coded FMCW laser-headlamp architecture has been proposed for integrated sensing, communication, and illumination \cite{liu2026laserheadlamp}. We use this physical-layer premise to study the scheduling consequences of mobility-dependent optical service rather than to claim experimental validation of a complete vehicular optical transceiver.

\subsection{Trajectory prediction in communication systems}
Jiang \emph{et al.} investigate trajectory prediction of a target light source for dynamic VLC with a narrow field of view \cite{jiang2020trajectory}. In RF V2V communication, Hajrasouliha and Shahgholi Ghahfarokhi use predicted vehicle locations in geo-based resource selection and report improvements in packet reception and blocking-related measures in their evaluated scenarios \cite{hajrasouliha2021trajectory}. Prediction has also been incorporated into vehicular computation systems, including trajectory-aware task offloading and predictive resource allocation \cite{guo2022trajectoryoffload,lu2024predictive}. Vehicle-state prediction itself is a mature estimation problem, with methods such as interacting multiple models combining alternative motion hypotheses \cite{gao2023imm}.

Our focus is downstream of the predictor. The quantity of interest is not trajectory error alone, but the packet utility obtained after the forecast passes through the scheduler. In the system studied here, the relevant sequence is
\[
\text{motion history}\rightarrow\text{future geometry}\rightarrow\text{link estimate}
\rightarrow\text{service decision}\rightarrow\text{packet outcome}.
\]
Information can be lost at any transition. In particular, a better estimate of future link quality has no incremental packet value when it leaves the service decision unchanged, or when changing that decision costs more than the future opportunity it preserves.

\section{System Model}
Time is divided into slots of duration 0.1 s. At slot $t$, the scheduler observes vehicle histories only up to $t$, together with queue state, packet deadlines, and previous service outcomes. A causal predictor estimates target positions over a horizon $H$. Predicted ego-relative range and bearing are mapped to optical-link quantities including SNR, BER/PER, goodput, outage state, and remaining link lifetime. The packet transmission in a slot is evaluated using the geometry realized in that slot. Future realized positions are never exposed to a deployable policy.

The optical model follows a PC-FMCW/DPSK physical-layer premise informed by phase-coded FMCW waveform studies \cite{kumbul2023smoothed,kumbul2023mimo} and the integrated laser-headlamp concept in \cite{liu2026laserheadlamp}. Its purpose is to provide a mobility-sensitive mapping from geometry to packet service. The mapping is analytical and is not calibrated against measured end-to-end vehicle-to-vehicle optical power; the study should therefore be interpreted as a system-level simulation rather than a hardware validation.

Packets arrive in a separate queue for each receiver. Failed transmissions may be retried while the packet remains valid. A deadline of $N$ slots gives a packet exactly $N$ opportunities for service, with the last admissible service slot at $arrival+N-1$. Only deadlines representable by an integer number of simulation slots are considered. Delivered packets, expirations, retransmissions, drops, and right-censored packets are tracked explicitly.

\section{Scheduling with Future Link Information}
Let $Q_t$ and $D_t$ denote the queue and deadline state at slot $t$, and let $\mathcal{H}_t$ contain the causal motion history. For receiver $i$, the predictive scheduler evaluates a utility of the form
\begin{equation}
U_i\!\left(\mathcal{H}_t,Q_t,D_t,\widehat{\mathcal{L}}_{i,t+1:t+H}\right),
\end{equation}
where $\widehat{\mathcal{L}}_{i,t+1:t+H}$ is the link evolution implied by the motion forecast. The scheduled receiver is
\begin{equation}
 i^{\star}=\arg\max_i U_i.
\end{equation}
A predicted loss of optical connectivity can increase a receiver's priority, allowing service to be advanced while the link is still usable.

This predictive preference is constrained by a current-service guard. Let $S_i(t)$ be the immediate service score and let $r$ denote the guard ratio. A forecast-driven alternative is allowed only if its immediate score retains the required fraction of the score associated with the reactive choice. The guard therefore controls how much current service quality may be exchanged for a predicted future opportunity. We examine $r\in\{0.8,0.9,0.95,1.0\}$ during development and select the setting according to a cross-regime criterion before holdout evaluation. The selected value is $r=1.0$, for which predictive reordering cannot replace the reactive choice with an action having a lower immediate score under the guard definition.

Random, Round Robin, Reactive Greedy, and Proportional Fair are retained as reactive references. The causal motion models include Last Position, constant velocity, constant acceleration, Kalman-CV, and IMM-style forecasting where applicable. Future-position information is used only for evaluator-side diagnostics. Such references indicate the information available under perfect future knowledge; they are not deployable and are not treated as globally optimal schedulers.

\section{Experimental Methodology}
\subsection{Operating conditions and data separation}
Scheduler parameters are developed using seeds 20270101--20270110. The primary evaluation uses a disjoint set of 20 paired seeds, 20270201--20270220. Each seed defines a common episode for the predictive and reactive policies, so differences are computed under matched traffic and mobility conditions.

Four operating regimes probe different sources of scheduling pressure: packet deadlines of 0.1 s and 0.5 s, offered load 1.1, and reference SNR +3 dB. The primary endpoint is the paired difference in packet goodput between the selected predictive policy and Reactive Greedy. The episode, rather than an individual packet or simulation time sample, is the unit of statistical inference.

\subsection{Statistical analysis}
For each regime, the mean paired goodput difference is accompanied by a 95\% percentile-bootstrap confidence interval based on 100,000 paired replicates. A two-sided paired Wilcoxon signed-rank test is reported, with Holm adjustment across the four primary comparisons. We additionally report paired Cohen $d_z$ and episode-level win, loss, and tie fractions.

A practical margin of $m=0.001$ Mbps is fixed for interpretation. A positive effect requires the lower confidence bound to exceed $+m$; a negative effect requires the upper bound to lie below $-m$. When neither condition is satisfied, the experiment does not establish a practically meaningful effect in either direction. This rule separates absence of evidence for a gain from evidence that two policies are equivalent.

The paired outcomes show that the small average differences arise in a setting dominated by ties. All 20 episodes tie at the 0.1 s deadline. At 0.5 s, 95\% tie and 5\% favor the predictive scheduler. Under offered load 1.1, 85\% tie, 10\% favor the predictive scheduler, and 5\% favor Reactive Greedy. At +3 dB, 80\% tie, 15\% favor the predictive scheduler, and 5\% favor Reactive Greedy. Thus, for the selected policy, future mobility information usually leaves episode-level goodput unchanged.

The all-zero 0.1 s comparison produces a degenerate Wilcoxon statistic; its raw test is undefined and the adjusted value is conservatively assigned $p=1$. Raw Wilcoxon $p$ values for the remaining regimes are 0.3173, 1.0, and 0.2733. After Holm adjustment, all four values are 1.0. Together with the confidence intervals and effect sizes in Table~\ref{tab:primary}, these results provide no statistical basis for claiming an incremental goodput advantage of the selected predictive scheduler.

\subsection{Effect of the service guard}
The development experiments reveal why a policy with access to future link information can behave much like a reactive one. At $r=0.8$, the forecast is allowed to alter service order more freely. Mean goodput then increases in the 0.5 s deadline and +3 dB regimes, but decreases under the 0.1 s deadline. This short-deadline loss violates the cross-regime selection requirement. The 0.9 and 0.95 settings fail the same requirement, leaving $r=1.0$ as the selected configuration.

The guard controls a genuine scheduling trade-off. A weaker guard can serve a receiver before a predicted link deterioration, but doing so may surrender a better transmission in the current slot. With a tight deadline, that immediate opportunity can be difficult or impossible to recover. At $r=1.0$, the scheduler avoids this sacrifice, yet the forecast can affect service only when a predictive alternative is already competitive in immediate score. The resulting policy is consequently robust to the observed short-deadline loss but has relatively few opportunities to depart from Reactive Greedy.

\subsection{Where predictive value disappears}
The holdout ties and the guard ablations point to the same boundary. Future information first has to alter the ranking of feasible service actions. A changed action must then improve a packet outcome rather than merely move service between slots or queues. Finally, the benefit of preserving a future link opportunity must exceed the opportunity cost created in the present. If the reactive and predictive choices coincide, or if the current queue/deadline state makes a forecast-driven alternative too expensive, the forecast has no incremental packet-level effect even though it may accurately describe future motion.

This interpretation does not imply that trajectory prediction is generally ineffective. The lower-guard experiments demonstrate that future information can change packet outcomes, including beneficial ones. What the primary evaluation shows is narrower: under the selected cross-regime protection of immediate service, those opportunities are too infrequent or too small to establish an incremental goodput gain over Reactive Greedy in the tested conditions.

\section{Discussion}
The results separate three quantities that are easy to conflate: forecast quality, decision change, and communication utility. Prediction quality concerns how well future motion or link state is estimated. Decision change concerns whether the additional information leads the scheduler to choose a different feasible action. Communication utility concerns whether that changed action ultimately delivers more useful traffic. Improvement in the first quantity does not guarantee improvement in the third.

This distinction is particularly important for deadline-constrained traffic. A link that is predicted to disappear creates pressure to serve its queue early, whereas an expiring packet creates pressure to serve whichever queue contains the most time-critical traffic. These two forms of urgency can agree, but they need not. The service guard mediates the conflict by limiting the immediate quality that can be sacrificed for a future opportunity. The experiments show the consequence of choosing either side too strongly: permissive predictive reordering can help some conditions while hurting a tight-deadline condition, whereas conservative protection can leave the predictive policy with little room to act.

The high tie fractions provide useful evidence about this interface. They show that the weak average effect is not simply the result of positive and negative changes cancelling across every episode. For most paired episodes, the final goodput is exactly the same. This is consistent with an actionability bottleneck in which additional information reaches the scheduler but rarely produces a service change with a measurable packet consequence. The present experiment does not directly instrument every prediction-induced action, so this mechanism should be interpreted as an explanation supported by the guard behavior and tie structure rather than as a complete causal decomposition.

For predictive scheduler design, this suggests that improving the motion predictor alone may have diminishing returns. A scheduler also needs to recognize the states in which future information is worth acting upon. Such a rule could account jointly for forecast confidence, predicted loss of a future service opportunity, queue occupancy, and remaining deadline slack. The objective would not be to make prediction influence more decisions indiscriminately, but to concentrate that influence on decisions for which the expected packet benefit exceeds the immediate service cost.

\section{Limitations}
The study is simulation based. Optical service is generated from analytical geometry and reference-SNR assumptions rather than from a measured end-to-end vehicular optical channel. The PC-FMCW/DPSK literature provides the physical-layer motivation, but the complete geometry-to-packet model has not been validated in a road deployment or hardware-in-the-loop experiment.

The primary evaluation contains four operating regimes and 20 paired holdout episodes per regime. It covers a finite family of schedulers and does not establish that the selected guard is globally optimal. Nor does it provide a dense map over mobility, traffic, SNR, deadline, and forecast-error conditions. The mechanism analysis is based on guard ablations and episode-level outcome ties; direct logging and classification of individual prediction-induced action changes would allow a stronger decomposition of when forecasts help or hurt.

Evaluation with measured optical channels, broader mobility and traffic distributions, learned trajectory models, larger driving datasets, and explicit decision-change instrumentation would test how far the observed actionability boundary extends beyond the present setting.

\section{Conclusion}
Future mobility information is useful to packet scheduling only when the scheduler can turn it into a better service decision. In the vehicular optical system studied here, the predictive policy selected to protect immediate service shows no established goodput advantage over Reactive Greedy across four primary operating regimes. Mean differences range from -0.00050 to +0.00080 Mbps, and 80--100\% of paired holdout episodes have identical goodput. Allowing the forecast to influence service more freely creates gains in some development conditions but also produces a loss when the packet deadline is shortest. The limiting factor is therefore not simply whether future motion can be predicted. It is whether the current queue, deadline, and link state leave a consequential action that the forecast can improve at acceptable immediate cost. Predictive vehicular schedulers should be designed around this decision boundary rather than treating forecast availability as a guarantee of communication gain.
