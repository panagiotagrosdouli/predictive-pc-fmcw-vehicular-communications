\title{When Does Future Mobility Information Have Packet-Level Value in Vehicular Optical Scheduling?}
\author{\IEEEauthorblockN{Panagiota Grosdouli}\IEEEauthorblockA{Department of Electrical and Computer Engineering\\School of Engineering\\Democritus University of Thrace\\Xanthi, Greece\\panagros1@ee.duth.gr}}

\begin{abstract}
Trajectory prediction can reveal how vehicular links are likely to evolve, but an accurate forecast is useful to a communication system only if it leads to a better service decision. This paper examines that distinction in a mobility-sensitive PC-FMCW/DPSK vehicular optical scheduler with queues and hard packet deadlines. Causal motion histories are converted into predicted geometry and optical-link evolution, which are then used for packet scheduling; future ground truth is reserved for evaluation. A deadline-discretization error identified during validation was corrected before the final study, after which the current-service guard was selected on fresh development seeds and evaluated once on a disjoint 20-seed synthetic holdout. The selected guard ratio is 1.0. Relative to Reactive Greedy, the mean goodput differences across the four pre-specified regimes are 0.00000, +0.00035, -0.00050, and +0.00080 Mbps. None satisfies the pre-specified criterion for a practically meaningful positive or negative effect, and exact paired ties account for 80--100\% of episodes. Development ablations explain the result: weaker guards allow the forecast to reorder service more often and can produce gains in some regimes, but they also introduce losses under the shortest deadline. The 1.0 guard avoids that sacrifice by preserving the best immediate service score, leaving little room for the forecast to change packet outcomes. The central result is therefore not that mobility prediction is intrinsically beneficial or useless, but that its packet-level value depends on whether future information can change a consequential scheduling decision without imposing a larger immediate service cost. All evidence is synthetic and model-based.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Vehicular optical communication offers a directional complement to RF V2X, but its performance is closely tied to relative geometry, field of view, alignment, and mobility \cite{memedi2021vehicular}. Recent phase-coded FMCW laser-headlamp work also points toward platforms in which sensing, illumination, and communication share optical hardware \cite{liu2026laserheadlamp,kumbul2023smoothed,kumbul2023mimo}. In such systems, knowledge of future vehicle motion appears naturally useful: a strong link may soon deteriorate, whereas a reactive scheduler can respond only to the opportunity available now.

The difficulty is that useful information and useful action are not the same thing. Trajectory prediction has already been studied in dynamic narrow-field-of-view VLC \cite{jiang2020trajectory}; vehicular VLC resource allocation has addressed connectivity, load balancing, and network lifetime \cite{msongaleli2020resource}; and predicted vehicle motion has been used for LTE-V2V resource selection and vehicular computation/resource allocation \cite{hajrasouliha2021trajectory,guo2022trajectoryoffload,lu2024predictive}. Low-latency and reliable vehicular resource allocation is likewise well established \cite{guo2019latency}. The open issue considered here is therefore more specific: after a mobility forecast is translated into link predictions and then into a scheduling action, does it actually change which packets are delivered before their deadlines?

That question separates several effects that are often conflated. A trajectory forecast may be accurate while adding little beyond the current link state. It may alter the ranking of receivers without changing the packet that is ultimately delivered. It may even identify a link that will soon disappear, yet serving that link early can be counterproductive if another queue has a stronger immediate opportunity or a more urgent packet. Prediction has communication value only when the information survives this chain from forecast to action to packet outcome.

We study this chain in a model-based PC-FMCW/DPSK vehicular optical setting with causal predictors, explicit queues and deadlines, paired episode-level inference, and a service guard chosen before holdout evaluation. Future ground-truth positions are used only on the evaluator side and are never available to deployable scheduling policies. The aim is to identify when future mobility information becomes actionable rather than to assume that prediction should outperform a strong current-state scheduler.

Three findings emerge. First, the development procedure selects a current-service guard of 1.0, which prevents a forecast-driven choice from accepting a lower immediate service score than Reactive Greedy. Second, on a disjoint 20-seed holdout, none of the four pre-specified operating regimes shows a practically established positive or negative goodput difference. Third, most paired episodes are exact ties. Ablations reveal why: relaxing the guard creates more opportunities for predictive reordering, but the resulting gains in some regimes are accompanied by a loss under the shortest deadline. The constraint that makes the policy robust also removes much of the freedom needed to exploit future information.

The contribution is thus a packet-level account of \emph{actionability}. We connect causal motion forecasting, predicted optical-link evolution, scheduling decisions, and realized packet outcomes in one auditable evaluation; report the paired holdout behavior of a prospectively selected guarded scheduler; and show how protection of immediate service can suppress both harmful and potentially beneficial forecast-driven reordering. This establishes a narrower and more useful design lesson than a general claim of predictive superiority.

\section{Related Work}
\subsection{Vehicular optical communication}
Vehicular VLC research establishes both the opportunity and the constraints created by directional vehicle lighting, line-of-sight geometry, mobility, and optical channel dynamics \cite{memedi2021vehicular}. Msongaleli and Kucuk formulate resource utilisation for VLC-based VANETs around network lifetime, connectivity, and load balancing, using an ILP and a heuristic for denser cases \cite{msongaleli2020resource}. Our focus is different: we follow mobility information through a packet scheduler with explicit deadlines and ask whether it adds utility beyond a strong current-state decision rule.

\subsection{Trajectory prediction as communication side information}
Trajectory prediction has already entered communication-aware vehicular systems. Jiang \emph{et al.} study target-light-source trajectory prediction for a dynamic VLC system with a narrow field of view \cite{jiang2020trajectory}. In RF V2V, Hajrasouliha and Shahgholi Ghahfarokhi use predicted vehicle locations for geo-based LTE-V2V resource selection and report improvements in packet reception and blocking-related metrics under their evaluated settings \cite{hajrasouliha2021trajectory}. Vehicular edge-computing studies have likewise coupled trajectory prediction with task offloading and resource allocation \cite{guo2022trajectoryoffload,lu2024predictive}. These studies motivate the use of mobility forecasts but do not answer the packet-level question considered here.

\subsection{Prediction quality versus downstream utility}
Vehicle trajectory forecasting is itself a mature problem; interacting-multiple-model methods, for example, combine motion hypotheses for interpretable prediction \cite{gao2023imm}. Communication performance, however, depends on what the scheduler can do with a forecast. The relevant chain is motion history $\rightarrow$ forecast $\rightarrow$ predicted link state $\rightarrow$ scheduling action $\rightarrow$ realized packet outcome. Better performance at an upstream stage does not guarantee a better outcome downstream. We evaluate this propagation directly and use future ground truth only as an evaluator-side information reference.

\subsection{Positioning}
Taken together, prior work covers dynamic VLC trajectory prediction \cite{jiang2020trajectory}, VLC-VANET resource allocation \cite{msongaleli2020resource}, trajectory-informed V2V resource selection \cite{hajrasouliha2021trajectory}, and predictive vehicular offloading/resource allocation \cite{guo2022trajectoryoffload,lu2024predictive}. The distinct question here is whether future mobility information remains \emph{actionable at packet level} once immediate service quality, queues, deadlines, and robustness across operating conditions are considered simultaneously.

\section{System Model}
At decision slot $t$, the scheduler observes vehicle histories only through $t$, together with packet queues, deadlines, and past service. A causal predictor produces target positions over horizon $H$. Ego-relative range and bearing are mapped through an analytical optical-link model to predicted SNR, BER/PER, goodput, outage, and link-lifetime estimates. Packet outcomes are evaluated using the realized geometry at the transmission slot; future realized positions are never supplied to deployable policies.

The physical-layer model follows the PC-FMCW/DPSK premise and reference parameters supported by phase-coded FMCW waveform and transceiver studies \cite{kumbul2023smoothed,kumbul2023mimo}. Laser-headlamp work provides additional motivation for integrated optical sensing, communication, and illumination \cite{liu2026laserheadlamp}. The complete geometry-to-link mapping remains analytical, however, and absolute received power is not calibrated against measured vehicle-to-vehicle optical power.

Packets enter per-receiver queues and may be retransmitted after unsuccessful service attempts. An $N$-slot physical deadline permits exactly $N$ service opportunities, with the final legal service index equal to $arrival+N-1$. Deadlines that cannot be represented exactly at the simulator slot duration are rejected rather than rounded. Queue accounting includes delivery, expiration, retransmission, drops, and right-censored packets.

\section{Scheduling and Information References}
For receiver $i$, the predictive scheduler evaluates
\begin{equation}
 i^{\star}=\arg\max_i U_i\!\left(\mathcal{H}_t,Q_t,D_t,\widehat{\mathcal{L}}_{i,t+1:t+H}\right),
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{H}_t$ denotes causal motion history, $Q_t$ and $D_t$ represent queue and deadline state, and $\widehat{\mathcal{L}}$ is the predicted link evolution. A receiver whose usable link is expected to disappear can therefore acquire additional urgency.

This creates a direct trade-off between future link urgency and immediate service quality. The current-service guard permits forecast-driven reordering only when the predictive candidate preserves a specified fraction of the immediate service score achieved by the reactive choice. Guard ratios 0.8, 0.9, 0.95, and 1.0 are evaluated during development. The selected ratio is 1.0; at this setting, a forecast-driven reordering cannot accept a lower immediate service score under the guard definition.

Reactive references include Random, Round Robin, Reactive Greedy, and Proportional Fair. Causal motion references include Last Position, constant velocity, constant acceleration, Kalman-CV, and IMM-style forecasting where configured. Future-position oracle diagnostics serve only as information references. They are not deployable policies and are not interpreted as globally optimal offline schedulers.

\section{Experimental and Statistical Protocol}
Validation of the simulator identified an off-by-one deadline error: an intended $N$-slot deadline had previously allowed $N+1$ service opportunities. A nominal 0.05 s condition was also incompatible with the 0.1 s simulation slot. Both issues were resolved before the final evaluation. The results reported here use the corrected deadline semantics; earlier affected runs are not used as evidence.

Development uses seeds 20270101--20270110. The holdout consists of disjoint seeds 20270201--20270220 and is evaluated only after guard selection. Four operating regimes are specified in advance: deadline 0.1 s, deadline 0.5 s, offered load 1.1, and reference SNR +3 dB. The primary endpoint is the paired difference in goodput between the guarded candidate and Reactive Greedy. The inferential unit is the paired scenario/episode seed rather than individual packets, windows, or time samples.

For each regime, the analysis uses 100,000 paired percentile-bootstrap replicates for the mean difference, a 95\% confidence interval, a two-sided paired Wilcoxon signed-rank test, Holm correction across the four comparisons, paired Cohen $d_z$, and paired win/loss/tie fractions. A practical margin of $m=0.001$ Mbps is specified in advance. A positive effect requires the confidence-interval lower bound to exceed $+m$; a negative effect requires the upper bound to fall below $-m$. All other outcomes are classified as neutral/uncertain. Failure to reject a null hypothesis is not interpreted as proof of equality.

At +3 dB, 80\% tie, 15\% favor the guarded policy, and 5\% favor Reactive Greedy. In most paired episodes, therefore, access to the mobility forecast leaves realized goodput unchanged.

These results support neither a practically established benefit nor a practically established loss, and they should not be read as proof that the policies are mathematically identical.

\subsection{RQ2: Why does future information fail to produce robust incremental utility?}
The development ablations expose a service-order trade-off. A guard of 0.8 produces positive mean goodput changes at the 0.5 s deadline and at +3 dB, but it also produces a negative mean at the 0.1 s deadline and therefore fails the pre-specified worst-regime noninferiority requirement. Guards 0.9 and 0.95 fail the same requirement. Only the 1.0 guard is eligible and is selected before the holdout is examined.

This pattern reveals an actionability bottleneck. Weaker guards let predicted link urgency alter service order more often, creating both opportunities and costs. The 1.0 guard prevents the scheduler from sacrificing the best immediate service score, but in doing so it removes much of the freedom through which future information could affect packet outcomes. The 80--100\% tie rates on the holdout are consistent with this mechanism: the forecast may contain information even when the scheduler has little room to act on it.

\subsection{RQ3: What does the result imply about the value of prediction?}
The final evidence does not support the earlier, pre-correction indication of improvement in three of four regimes. Nor does it support the opposite conclusion that prediction is generally useless. The development ablations show that forecast-driven reordering can change outcomes, but the direction depends on the operating regime. Under the robustness criterion used here, the policy that avoids unacceptable cross-regime loss does not establish an incremental goodput advantage on the holdout.

The distinction between information and utility is therefore central. Future geometry may be informative, link prediction may be accurate, and a predictive ranking may differ from a reactive ranking. Packet-level value appears only when that information changes a service decision and the realized benefit of that change exceeds its opportunity cost elsewhere in the queue and deadline system.

\section{Discussion}
\subsection{Prediction accuracy is not communication value}
Trajectory forecasting studies commonly emphasize geometric prediction quality, while resource-allocation studies evaluate performance under a chosen optimization objective \cite{gao2023imm,hajrasouliha2021trajectory,guo2022trajectoryoffload,lu2024predictive}. The present results show why these levels should be kept separate. A scheduler consumes a forecast through an action-selection rule; the forecast acquires packet value only through the action it changes and the packet outcome that follows.

\subsection{Link urgency and packet urgency need not coincide}
Predicted link deterioration creates \emph{link urgency}; approaching packet expiration creates \emph{packet urgency}. The two need not rank receivers in the same order. Serving a link because it will soon disappear can be harmful when another queue offers a stronger immediate transmission opportunity or contains a more urgent packet. The observed guard-selection pattern is consistent with this tension, although the present experiment does not establish a universal quantitative relationship between the two forms of urgency.

\subsection{Why a neutral result is informative}
The development rule deliberately rejects a predictor that gains in one regime by creating an unacceptable loss in another. The policy that survives this requirement is robust, but its realized primary behavior is close to Reactive Greedy. This suggests a concrete design direction: trajectory prediction alone is not enough. A predictive scheduler also needs a reliable way to identify the moments at which forecast information is both trustworthy and valuable enough to justify changing immediate packet service.

\section{Limitations}
The study is based on simulation and analytical channel assumptions. It does not include a road deployment, measured end-to-end vehicular optical channel, or hardware-in-the-loop experiment. The PC-FMCW/DPSK literature provides physical-layer motivation and parameter provenance, but it does not validate the complete geometry-to-packet model used here.

The experiments cover a finite scheduler family and four primary operating regimes. The selected 1.0 guard is not claimed to be globally optimal. The study also does not provide a dense map of the operating region, a calibrated sensitivity curve over forecast error, or a globally optimal oracle upper bound. Consequently, the mechanism interpretation is strongest for the observed guard-selection behavior and the paired tie structure reported here.

Learned trajectory models and large-scale driving-dataset experiments are outside the scope of this study. External validity should be tested further with measured optical channels, broader mobility conditions, additional traffic regimes, and independent data.

\section{Conclusion}
Future mobility information is valuable to a vehicular scheduler only when it changes a decision that matters. In the PC-FMCW/DPSK optical scheduling model studied here, development selection favors a 1.0 current-service guard. On a disjoint 20-seed synthetic holdout, none of four pre-specified goodput comparisons against Reactive Greedy establishes a practically meaningful positive or negative effect, and exact paired ties account for 80--100\% of episodes. The ablations explain this behavior: allowing more forecast-driven reordering creates both potential gains and cross-regime losses, whereas protecting immediate service removes most of the scheduler's freedom to exploit the forecast. The broader design lesson is therefore conditional but clear: prediction becomes packet-level value only when the scheduler can act on it at the right moment and the benefit of doing so exceeds the immediate queue and deadline cost.
